From the first frame _EEAAO_ grabs the viewer’s attention. The opening credit sequence alone runs through what seems like dozens of versions of the Gozie Agbo production company logo and sets expectations for the viewer. That initial shock is only the beginning of the visual overwhelm to come while preparing us to see the world through the eyes of Evelyn Wang, the main character of the film. If the opening sequence has that many variations of a single logo, what are we getting ourselves into?

The first shot is an expertly framed Wang family singing karaoke in the reflection of a circular mirror. The mirror is on a desk covered in papers, figurines of raccoons, and tons of clutter. We're seeing a reflection of who each family member is and learn the family dynamic in seconds. 

On the left of the reflection is Waymond, husband to Evelyn and father of Joy. He's focused on having fun in the moment and keeping everyone engaged in singing. 

Far in the background audio mix we can faintly hear Joy, who’s standing in the middle of the mirror, say she hasn’t “sung that song since high school.” Whatever is being sung, she’s embarrassed by it. Joy is feeling exposed and embarassed about engaging with things she loves, or once loved. She seems uncomfortable being around her parents. 

Evelyn, holding her place on the right, decides to mute Joy by placing a hand over Joy’s mouth. We don't know it, but the song has cuss words and sexual innuendo which Evelyn hasn’t accepted as part of Joy’s life yet. In an act of overbearing parenting, Evelyn decides to exert control over Joy. 

As viewers we don’t know anyone’s names yet, but on our own reflection we can understand the visiaul metaphor at play: Evelyn tries to keep her own joy under control as much as possible. It's what she thinks being a responsible adult is.

The end of this shot confirms: we’ve gone from an elevated, special moment between family members back down to reality. The chaotic life of the Wangs is filled with messy piles of paperwork, loud background noises, and multiple tasks running at the same time. Evelyn seems to be trying to manage it all herself, even as Waymond offers to help and picks up a few pieces in the background. Evelyn runs the laundry business, helps care for her ungrateful, aging father, and attempts to maintain her marriage. 

The reality is that Evelyn __overextends__ herself to the point that nothing is actually getting done. She’s doing paperwork, chatting online, cooking breakfast (or more accurately, telling Waymond to cook it), and dealing with a nagging husband who won’t seem to leave her alone. We’re getting to see what a more average day in the life of the Wangs is like, far removed from a night of karaoke. 

Evelyn’s inability to focus challenges Waymond who has been trying desperately to get her attention. He grabs a receipt out of her hand in a way he finds playful but firm (another mirror on the right of the frame lets us see his face and reaction to know that’s the case). Waymond remarks that he knows "better than to ask to help you” to Evelyn. This makes it obvious that her tendency to think she knows everything or can take care of things herself is well established. 

Waymond cooking breakfast is the only available way he can engage with her. He tries to find space in their conversation for his needs to be met and they have nothing to do with the tasks at hand. Amidst her attempts at multitasking, Evelyn wrongly gets the impression that Waymond wants to give her more paperwork to file for taxes. Waymond quickly grabs the papers out of her hands before she gets a chance to read them. 

Waymond acknowledges that Evelyn is trying to get everything done so that she can make the Chinese New Year party they have planned for later that night perfect. He knows Evelyn is trying to impress her father but she's dismissive of his acknowledgement.

Waymond tries to remind her that she’s helped nurture their family and business but Evelyn responds that it’s crazy to think that’s what her father will see. Waymond asks her if that’s what the two of them see together. She looks around quietly, seeming unconvinced herself. Evelyn feels a need to impress her father, and Waymond wants to know if the two of them have created something _they're_ proud of. He knows Evelyn's father Gong-Gong will see that clearly if it's the truth, and it will give Waymond emotional footing he can keep close in relationship to Evelyn.

A yell from the other room and a quick nod of Evelyn’s head later, she rushes off to the laundromat downstairs where a door is buzzing. Waymond, dejected, stands in the living room and sighs. He realizes the divide between him and his wife is great, maybe too much to traverse. He looks down at the paperwork he’s been trying to discuss with his wife: he’s desperately trying to serve Evelyn divorce papers. All of the effort he has been putting into the morning has been him trying to connect with his wife. At every turn, she has proven to be unavailable.

Waymond’s need for Evelyn to focus only serves to solidify his choice of divorce. Within the first 3 minutes we’ve learned a lot about how the Wang family interacts: 
* There’s poor communication between family members
* A lack of mutual interest or care
* An inability to meet one another’s needs
* There’s censorship, rigid morality, a chaotic home, and everyone is stretched too thin.

Waymond’s decision to end his marriage seems completely reasonable given what we know. He’s unable to cope with the reality of his situation any longer. It seems like he wants a way out. 

The way Evelyn lives keeps her so invested in herself and her work that her relationships suffer to the point of breaking. We’ve all stretched ourselves too thin from time to time. In our modern world especially, this “thinning” of attention has become a near-universal experience. There are SO MANY different demands on our time. How does anyone choose what to focus on? How can we keep ourselves well enough to accomplish anything?
