∞ What Rock Jobu has touched on here is a concept most famously introduced to the general public by world famous astronomer Carl Sagan and his wife, the writer and producer Ann Druyan in their book Pale Blue Dot called The Great Demotions. The bubble metaphor isn’t a hollow one; the theory that Sagan is asserting in her essay is called Bubble Theory. 

∞ How is the universe like a bubble? Alan Guth, professor of theoretical physics at MIT, and several colleagues introduced what they called Cosmic Inflation theory in 1979. The group had been contemplating the creation of the universe known as the Big Bang. The idea that the universe as we know it today started from an extremely small, dense origin point and rapidly expanded holds weight for several reasons. First, the Big Bang is verifiable when General Relativity is applied. General Relativity is the idea that the gravity of bodies in motion bend and warp spacetime around them, as well as how bodies in space warp light. A quote by John Wheeler, himself a famous theoretical physicist, explains the idea succinctly: “Matter tells space how to curve, and space tells matter how to move.” Second is the Cosmological Principle. How did everything in the universe get to where it is? The Cosmological Principle says that matter is more or less evenly distributed throughout the universe when viewed at a large enough scale because matter is isotropic, or has a physical property that when measured in different directions has the same value.

∞ It boils down to this: the Big Bang was a rapid expansion that shot matter out over the vast, former emptiness of space, causing that matter to distribute in all directions at once, and because of several now-known laws of motion and gravity that matter eventually slowed its movement. All of that matter “settled” into different areas of space because of the gravity of other bodies that had bent and warped spacetime around them. With the Big Bang explanation comes a few problems that Guth’s group were trying to resolve. One: the universe when seen at scale is nearly flat. If the Big Bang shot matter out into all directions at the same time, the easy assumption to make is that our universe should look more like a sphere. Two: Cosmic Microwave Background Radiation is radiation that blankets the entire universe and has a nearly uniform temperature from every place its been measured. It’s even visible as an all-encompassing glow throughout space using microwave detection equipment. If we use the standardized principles of Big Bang expansion, the temperature of the radiation from two sections of the universe far enough apart from each other shouldn’t be the same. It would be impossible for that to be true, because the time it would take for light to travel from those two measured points would be older than the universe itself. Three: the Big Bang predicts the existence of magnetic monopoles that would have affected the distribution of matter throughout the universe because of their magnetic pull. Those magnetic monopoles have never been observed.

∞ Guth and his team resolve all of these issues by applying Cosmic Inflation. One: the problem with thinking of the universe as a sphere is a matter of scale. It’s easy to consider that the universe should be enclosed if we could observe it from the outside. However, as it stands right now, we can’t do that. We have never reached the “edge” of the universe as we know it and don’t know what, if anything, lies beyond that edge. Because of this, the universe being nearly flat makes sense. The universe expanded so far outward that the curve becomes almost imperceptible to us. Is it possible that our universe really is a sphere? Unless we can see it at tremendous scale, we’ll never know. Two: the temperature of the cosmic microwave background radiation being the same throughout the universe is sensical. It means that prior to Cosmic Inflation those parts of the universe were much closer together than the Big Bang could predict with its own ruleset. Inflation predicts the matter in the universe to have burst outward with exponential expansion and the only way the radiation temperature could be uniform is if the matter before that expansion was in close enough proximity, known as causal contact. Three: Cosmic Inflation allows for the idea of magnetic monopoles as long as they existed prior to inflation. As matter expanded, monopoles would have dissipated and become so rare as to be nearly undetectable.
