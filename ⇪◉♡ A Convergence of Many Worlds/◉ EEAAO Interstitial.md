From the very start, EEAAO demands the viewer’s attention. The opening credit sequence alone runs through so many versions of the Gozie Agbo production company logo that one can’t help but be shocked by it. That shock is only the beginning of the visual overwhelm to come. More than that, it sets us up as viewers to see the world through Evelyn’s eyes in a subversive way. If the film can’t even concentrate on a single logo to use, what more is in store for us as audience:observer?

The first shot of the movie itself prepares us for just that. While the Wang family sings karaoke, we watch them through the reflection of the circular mirror in the family home. Very literally, we see a reflection of who they all are as people and learn the family dynamic.     

On the left of the reflection is Waymond, focusing on having fun in the moment and keeping everyone engaged in singing. Far in the background audio mix we can faintly hear Joy, who’s standing in the middle of the mirror, say she hasn’t “sung that song since high school.” Whatever is being sung, she’s embarrassed by it. Joy is being challenged by herself and outside forces to be true to what she enjoys. 

But Evelyn, holding her place on the right, decides to mute Joy by placing a hand over Joy’s mouth. The song has cuss words and sexual innuendo, something Evelyn hasn’t accepted as part of Joy’s life:reality yet. To keep herself safe from deeper knowledge and understanding of who her daughter is, Evelyn decides that control is the answer she’d rather have and exerts it over Joy. We don’t know anyone’s names yet, but on our own reflection we realize the visual metaphor is also a literal one. Evelyn tries to keep her own concept of joy under control as much as possible.

The camera continues to move towards the mirror then gently comes to rest. The score in the background reaches a high strain and stops suddenly. The lighting in the room changes, it’s daytime. We hear the sound of a door opening that then slams shut with so much force that the mirror we’ve been looking at falls slightly downward, revealing Evelyn running toward a dining room table filled with paperwork in a poorly organized, chaotic space. We’ve gone from an elevated, special moment with family  back down to reality. We’re getting to see what a more average day in the life of the Wangs is like.

Evelyn Wang is a woman possessed:obsessed with being everything she can to both herself and to her family. She runs a small business. She helps care for her ungrateful, aging father. Though she tries to keep a handle on everything that happens to her her family in reality Evelyn has a grip on nothing. She overextends herself to the point that nothing is actually getting done. She’s doing paperwork, chatting online, cooking breakfast (or more accurately, telling Waymond to cook it), and dealing with a nagging husband who won’t seem to leave her alone! 

Evelyn’s inability to focus challenges Waymond who’s trying desperately to get her attention. He grabs a receipt out of her hand in a way he finds playful (another mirror on the right of the frame lets us see his face and reaction to know that’s the case).  Waymond remarks here about “knowing better than to ask to help you” to Evelyn. This makes it obvious that her tendency to think she knows everything or can take care of things herself is well established. 

Waymond quickly gives in to his wife’s demands by cooking breakfast as a way to engage with her. He tries to find space in their conversation for his needs to be met. Whatever he’s trying to talk about seems very important. He’s even trying to make a specific meeting time so they can concentrate on it. Amidst her attempts at multitasking, Evelyn gets the impression that Waymond wants to give her more paperwork. Waymond quickly grabs it out of her hands before she gets a chance to read it. 

Clearly trying to make some kind of connection, Waymond remarks that he knows Evelyn wants to make everything perfect for the Chinese New Year party they have planned for later that night. Evelyn is trying to impress her father. Her husband tries to remind her that she’s helped nurture their family and business. Evelyn says that it’s crazy to think that’s what her father will see. Waymond asks her if that’s what the two of them see. She looks around quietly, seeming unconvinced herself. A yell from the other room and a quick nod of Evelyn’s head later, she rushes off to the laundromat downstairs where a door is buzzing. Waymond, dejected, stands in the living room and sighs. He realizes the divide between them both is great. He looks down at the paperwork he’s trying to discuss with his wife: he’s desperately trying to serve Evelyn divorce papers.

The irony of the situation is that Waymond’s need for Evelyn to focus only serves to solidify his choice more and more. Within the first 3 minutes we’ve learned so much about how the Wang family interacts. There’s poor communication, lack of mutual interest or care, an inability to meet one another’s needs. There’s censorship, rigid morality, a chaotic home, everyone is stretched too thin. Waymond’s decision to end his marriage seems completely reasonable. He’s unable to cope with the reality of his situation any longer. He wants a way out. 

Evelyn’s way of living is all too familiar to me. She’s trying to juggle everything for herself and her family, making last minute “big push” efforts to complete major tasks:projects like doing the family taxes the day they’re due, being so invested in herself:her work that her relationships suffer to the point of breaking… 

We’ve all stretched ourselves too thin from time to time. In our modern world especially, this “thinning” of attention has become a near-universal experience. There are SO MANY different demands on our time. How does anyone choose what to focus on? How can we keep ourselves well enough to accomplish anything?
