As we grow up we’re taught how to move, speak, live, and think. A variety of factors including socioeconomic status, nationality, race, and ability level are shaped by our caregivers to help build our understanding of the world. As children, we accept our caregivers’ portrayal as 

reality or truth which can become a bubble that insulates us from opinions or experiences that don't align with those of our caregivers. Many of us stay content within the bubbles we’re born into. We often don’t have room to move within their hazy, rainbow-reflective, translucent edges. We can see beyond them in glimpses caught in grocery stores, at school, and on television. Our parental figures (if we’re so lucky) may attempt to give us insights into life as they understand it. Often, caregivers will see anything that might pop that bubble as a threat and remove the child’s ability to engage. Since most of us aren’t interested in biting the hand(s) that feeds us, we don’t question their authority until our teenage years. These people are all we have ever known of someone who (hopefully) is kind, understanding, and giving. A caregiver is an inherently trustworthy source of knowledge. At least, as children, we think so.

The resulting consciousness we form is a “coming together” of concepts and ideas from multiple sources ranging from within the home and beyond it. Things like the interactions we have with classmates can shape our insights by letting us see small glimpses of how other people live. When we watch TV, we’re shown certain standards of living and how people interact and deal with each other. In our youth, we’re always learning. Our minds are sponges that feed on information, no matter what direction it’s coming from. We might soak and soak without so much as a second thought as critical thinking is a learned skill that may not have been taught. 

RAW introduces a concept in the first pages of PR from Dr. Leonard Orr: The Thinker and The Prover. In short, whatever the Thinker thinks, the Prover proves.

“The Thinker can think about virtually anything[...] comparative religion and philosophy show that the Thinker can regard itself as mortal, as immortal, as both mortal and immortal…or even as nonexistent…it can think itself into living in a Christian universe, a Marxist universe, a scientific-relativistic universe, or a Nazi universe – among many other possibilities.”

The point being, the power the human mind has over the brain and body is often greater than the sum of its bodies’ parts. As much as our bodies act as one unit, each individual part of our bodies can be focused on internally. No part of the body works entirely on its own. Different muscles, tendons, and nerves control our individual body parts but the signal for control comes from the mind and moves through the physical brain. 

When we think of moving a hand to grab a pencil for example, it is our mind that’s sending a signal to move it. The brain interprets this signal, and the correct body parts respond. We are only as whole as we think we are at any given moment. This “wholeness” reflects how we think of ourselves. The strength of our body can sometimes be a reflection of the strength of our mind. However for people with neurodivergence, physical disabilities, or other ailments, that’s not necessarily the case. There are many reasons this connection may not work as expected or not exist. None of those reasons are a reflection of personal weakness.

Based on information taken in over the course of a life, any one of us can think of ourselves as the greatest being that has ever lived or as the lowliest slug unworthy of love, attention, or care. Our mortality or immortality are decided by us. The universe we decide to inhabit is chosen by us. 

And whatever the Thinker thinks, the Prover proves.

Whatever perceptions of the world the Thinker provides, the Prover will take all information, internal and external, to shape the fit and form of the thoughts provided. Do you think of the Earth as flat? Then you’re going to find all the information you can to make it flat in your mind and form patterns of thought and behavior to fit that. Are you a good follower of your religion, obeying every law and being the best adherent you can be? Then your deity’s love will come over you like the breaking crests of the ocean waves and you’ll be awash in it.

“Of course,” RAW chides, “it is fairly easy to see that other people’s minds operate this way; it is comparatively much harder to become aware that one’s own mind is working that way also.”

Our minds are powerful tools that can be used in many different ways. They have the power to believe what they want based on the evidence they gather and decide what the truth is. This applies to all people, everywhere. Each of us possess the same power to choose how we operate internally. Our ability to be objective is entirely subjective. 

For example, politics are no more objective than science, RAW argues, due to personal passions held by politicians and a limited view of objectivity. Views on political authority are subjective based on individual preferences. Some people are willing to let someone else have all the power and control over them. These people don’t want the responsibility of choosing how to live their life. When someone else makes the rules, that means there’s a guide to follow. Other people want little or no political authority to influence them. Wherever a political authority extends its reach, these people find ways to keep that reach at bay. 

With science, we sometimes try to create experiments to get an answer we want to be true. Experiments are created to try to prove something, in the hopes that the answer we want is correct. Think of my earlier example of the street light turning from red to green. How objective do you think my own experiments with consciousness are?  It’s important to understand that this kind of experimentation is flawed and plays into bias. None of us are free from the influence of bias and how it can alter our perceptions. We’re always on the lookout for truth, but bias can influence that information with our own version of the trut. Bias has a role to play in all experimentation. It’s why there are different ways to study and experiment in order to reduce bias. But the ultimate goal of science is the pursuit of objective truth.

Our hopes for an objective truth come from subjective assumptions:wants. All of these things are driven by passions. All “great” people (that is, those who have authority or are revered in some way as a positive ‘other’) are driven by their passions. They do what they must to make themselves an authority on whatever their passions are. People take the time to become the “best” versions of themselves through whatever means necessary in order to become self-assured and fulfilled. Because of that, there can be no truly “objective” view of life on the individual:singular level. Our minds work based on what we try to prove based on how we think. That becomes our reality, our chosen universe.

∞ RAW cites an example using Bell’s Theorem, a part of quantum theory. He uses the example of Bell’s Theorem this way: “In the area of extra-sensory perception, the results are uniform after more than a century: everybody who sets out to prove ESP exists succeeds, and everybody who sets out to prove that ESP does not exist also succeeds.”
	
Sometimes called the “sixth sense,” extra-sensory perception or ESP is the purported way people can gain information about their environment without any use of the other five senses of sight, sound, smell, touch, or taste. ESP asserts that the mind itself is a sensing apparatus, despite not having a physical form. Science as we know it doesn’t have a way to observe or measure anything that doesn’t have a measurable form, so ESP is controversial simply because some people think it exists, and some don’t. ESP is yet another example of the Thinker and the Prover. 

∞ Invoking Bell’s Theorem this way is a shorthand for RAW to explain quantum mechanics' relationship with observation. Observing quantum particles, according to some theories, changes the outcome of experiments meant to measure their location. Human consciousness and its expectations might also change the outcome. Trying to force a result changes the outcome. 

What we observe becomes our reality if we think our observations are correct. When we find a way to prove our observation is correct, the dialectic is complete. We’ve created a new reality for ourselves based on what we think and prove is true to ourselves.
