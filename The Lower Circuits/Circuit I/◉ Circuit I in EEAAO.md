Where i voluntarily disengaged from my caregivers on an intellectual level through physical engagement, we learn that Evelyn disengaged from her caregivers on a physical level with no outside element present. Evelyn as an adult still seeks her father’s approval at the start of the film.
	
All of this is shown during Evelyn’s Alternative Life Path Scan in the elevator of the IRS office. Her father’s disappointment in her is immediate due to the factors of her birth beyond anyone’s control: Evelyn being a girl is something the delivering doctor apologizes for. As a child, she is reprimanded for playing with boys by her father. In time she finds Waymond: first in school then later in life when he asks her to move with him to America. Here, Evelyn disengages from Gong-Gong: her moving away means an abandonment of her family and the duties that come with it. She has chosen individualism and so Gong-Gong leaves her with the consequences of that decision. Evelyn’s family abandons her. She attempts contact throughout her time in American via phone call but Gong-Gong’s disconnection is total, leaving Evelyn depressed and directionless.

One thing becomes abundantly clear throughout the Life Path Scan:flashback section of the film: Evelyn allows life to happen to her instead of taking control of her life. Other people’s decisions become her decisions. And when she does attempt control, she does so over other people (namely Joy). This overextension is met with backlash and drives her own family away (Joy physically moving out, Waymond attempting divorce).

As Evelyn experiences her past, we see the major events of her life, the things that shaped her. No part is insignificant. At birth there’s already disappointment thanks to cultural norms and gender expectations. Her being assigned female at birth causes Gong-Gong to sink into his own sadness. Seeing Evelyn play with boys in the neighborhood, we see her briefly being joyful. It’s cut short however when Gong-Gong chides her for running with sticks and playing in a masculine way (despite his personal wish that she was born male). 

We see Evelyn drawing in class instead of paying attention to her studies. Here we meet Waymond for the first time. The only reason he gives her attention is because of his interest in her art. Plus, as far as we know, this is the first time any male has given her any significant signs of encouragement or positive attention. The jump from early schooling to presumably early 20’s makes much more sense given this larger context. The likelihood of Evelyn having emotionally attached herself to Waymond because of his interest alone is extremely high. It may have been the only positive reinforcement she ever knew.

In her main timeline, Evelyn barely spends time thinking of the consequences of her actions; she agrees to join Waymond in America and to join him in his dream. Does she even have any of her own? Even so she’s trepidatious, She’s aware there will be consequences but acts anyway. 

Once in America, Evelyn and Waymond tour their new lives: the apartment, the laundromat. Evelyn’s disdain is palpable. The grimace on her face makes it clear how much regret she feels. We know now that her life isn’t what she expected.

And while at first she seems to find some happiness in having a child (imbued with so much hope she even names her Joy!) it’s clearly not enough. Redecorating the business, spending time with Waymond, raising Joy… before anything else we know that not having Gong-Gong even answer a single phone call is what weighs on her mind the most. It keeps Evelyn stuck in Circuit I (and Circuit II, more on that later) regarding him. She’s afraid of her own life because she moved towards safety at every opportunity, causing her great amounts of stress and anxiety.

Most people today cannot escape the imposed hell of individualism. The Wangs are no exception. The impetus of Waymond & Evelyn’s move out of China to America is chasing the hope of the “American Dream.” Waymond believes in himself, believes in some kind of goal for his life, and wants Evelyn to join him. Despite the culture he was raised in, he has adopted another reality in which all his (and Evelyn’s) dreams come true. Sadly, due to the system on which this “American Dream” is built, the Wang’s never go beyond bio-survival. Evelyn takes the brunt of the hit, becoming the person we initially come to know at the start of the film.

Waymond isn’t truly to blame here. He’s intelligent, capable, and willing to fight against the stacked deck of a fundamentally broken system. He has bought into propaganda. If the “American Dream” is a system by which the commoner lives in constant fear of not having enough to survive, not having anyone to rely on aside from themselves, and a State that will only begrudgingly aid the commoner as long as one lives by their complex rule set, then Waymond has succeeded beyond his wildest imagination. 

But we know that’s not the case. Waymond’s initial conversation with Evelyn isn’t just about the state of his marriage. Waymond is admitting to a total failure that encompasses the totality of the life he’s tried to build. His business with Evelyn is failing:has failed. He no longer feels a sense of security from it. The failure is still largely in his hands, however. By focusing on the mechanical:personable side of the business and entrusting Evelyn with the administration, he damned them all because he didn’t understand the limits of his wife’s abilities or her strengths:limits. He takes the blame, makes excuses, and somehow talks his way out of the most daunting situations. In this way, Waymond’s Circuit I is a boon; he has learned to survive in a way Evelyn initially can’t.

Despite having built her own family, Evelyn doesn’t see them for who they are but who she wants:expects them to be. It’s why Waymond tries so hard to connect but he grows distant to Evelyn in his heart. It’s why Evelyn tries so hard to control Joy but Joy pulls away and even leaves her behind (at this point if it’s not obvious how much of a metaphor Joy’s name is, I don’t know what more to say). 

Eventually Evelyn’s ask is answered, but not in a way anyone expects: she is called to bring her father to America. Gong-Gong’s health has deteriorated so much that he needs around the clock care. And so at the end of the Life Path Scan we’re caught up to the current timeline, all the while on some level Evelyn’s recap has brought her into the greater multiverse by force.
