i was born under a different name in Lakewood, a city in Southern California that isn’t known for much aside from being close to Long Beach. Growing up initially was plush. My parents were given a beautiful house to live in with me and, two years, two months, and eleven days later, my sister. The house was given to my family by my grandparents on my Dad’s side who i call Nana and Tata. Even when given such a massive leg up for a successful life, we had our struggles as all families do. Life was far from stable, though my parents did what they could to give the illusion of stability. 

My parents were in their mid-twenties when they married on Flag Day 1986. i was in utero as part of the ceremony. Any semblance of true emotional health between the two of them was suspect at best. Only a few months earlier my uncle (Dad’s brother) had taken his life at 29 on Cinco de Mayo in the garage of the house Nana and Tata gave to us. No doubt my father was still reeling from the shock of his loss as he walked down the aisle. My mother, love her, had never learned to process any of the emotional turmoil of her life. Most notably my mother wore the death of her father like a chip on her shoulder. He passed when she was eight years old. His absence surely stuck out to her that day.

As both of them were already multiple drug users and mutual physical abusers in the years leading up to the marriage, neither of them truly had the awareness or focus to raise children, much less care for themselves. The pressures of their mutual misunderstandings regarding life in general, coupled with the expectations of their parents and families, added even more stress to their relationship. My conception threw an even bigger wrench into the works of their dynamic. As i was conceived out of wedlock, my raised-Catholic father “did the right thing” and married my mother so they could raise me together.

When i was born, i wasn’t exactly healthy or on-time. Having been born a month premature, there were bound to be a few complications. From an outside perspective, my body and physicality seemed fine. My weight was average, my limbs and extremities intact. It wasn’t until ten months later that my seizures began.

As it turns out, the right hemisphere of my brain was drastically overdeveloped. The number of neural connections on that side was nearly double the average. Without major medical intervention, i was likely to suffer seizures for the rest of my life. Every indication was confirming that my life would have been cut drastically short without help.

My parents, blessed at the time with good health insurance, were able to take me to a pediatrician at a cutting-edge facility full of forward thinkers and top of the line equipment. Once my ailment was discovered, treatment was prescribed right away. For medication i was given a barbiturate, a drug meant for heavy sedation that’s closely related to benzodiazepine. Essentially, the plan was to numb my consciousness so completely that it would cause some of those connections in my brain to atrophy with the intent of slowing this rapid growth.

The second part of my treatment, as though the first part wasn’t unconventional enough, was more experimental still. The pediatrician prescribed me exposure therapy to stimulus via video games. The idea was that as connections in my brain dwindled, i should be exposed to engrossing things that challenged me beyond my abilities per age development expectations. Add to that a healthy exposure to flashing lights and sudden motion, my brain would in time learn to withstand the stresses of daily life in the rapidly developing modern world. The hope was that my brain would keep and develop meaningful connections based on how it needed to react to the artificial problems it would regularly face.

And so, under doctor’s orders, my parents bought a Nintendo Entertainment System. Heralded at the time as the cutting edge itself in home gaming entertainment, my time with the NES was highly monitored at the beginning. Since such a medical treatment wasn’t standard practice at the time (is it now?) all of the treatment was unconventional. 

Starting at just over one year old, I was playing video games for 30 minutes a day with full parental supervision and encouragement. The choice of games my parents rented and bought reflected my young age. Sesame Street learning games, Super Mario Bros., and Duck Hunt would be changed out periodically with rentals of Bugs Bunny’s 50th Birthday Blowout, Little Nemo, and Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles.

What started as thirty minutes of monitored use turned into an hour, then an hour unmonitored, and before anyone knew it, what was once medical advice became a hobby:infatuation:love. i had a new kind of drug to stimulate my brain: a digital opiate. It kept me distracted and docile while developing my brain to connect in ways that might be helpful to me in the future.

Over the years my dose of barbiturate grew along with me as did my taste in games. And as i grew up, my family grew apart. My parents' multiple drug use turned into frequent physical and mental abuse on both their parts. Their constant arguments over anything and everything affected all four of us, my sister included. My needs for attention were met by my mother who was staying at home to care for me. Mom made a point to help me develop my academic skills before i was enrolled in kindergarten. By the time i was three i was already reading, writing, and doing math at a first grade level. She wanted me to be capable, normal, to fit in. What she did instead was make me exceptional. And that exceptionality became what’s now known as a Gifted Child.
