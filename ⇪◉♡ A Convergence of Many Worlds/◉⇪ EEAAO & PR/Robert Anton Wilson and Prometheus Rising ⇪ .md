Born in January 1932, Robert Anton Wilson (RAW) grew up in Brooklyn, New York and was raised as a Catholic. A sickly child, throughout his youth RAW contracted several diseases including polio. However, he was able to find what he claimed to be a cure by practicing “the Sister Kenny Method” of treatment for polio. At the time, most people were made to stay immobile when suffering with this disease. The Sister Kenny Method instead focused on using hot packs to warm:ready muscles for use, followed by a specific series of exercises meant to strengthen those muscles. For RAW the treatment worked, and he was able to use his entire body in a more meaningful and complete way once again. Most medical professionals during RAW’s youth considered this treatment to have no basis in medical truth. It was the first time RAW challenged the status quo, already pushing back against established science as a child. It wouldn’t be the last.

RAW’s upbringing in Catholicism had no appeal to him as a person. He attended primarily Catholic schools until he was able to convince his parents otherwise. He wound up attending a Polytechnic high school after convincing them he was interested in engineering. It worked. With his newfound freedom, RAW made a point of visiting the school’s library often, consuming the words of people he never would have come across otherwise. He expanded his worldview with the works of poets, philosophers, alternative historians, and fiction writers. The highlight for him was discovering James Joyce who remained a favorite throughout his life. Other notable people include Korzybski, Hume, Jung, Keats, Yeats, Faulkner, and Steinbeck to name just a few. 

When it came time for college, RAW continued his engineering pursuits, focusing on electrical engineering specifically. Eventually he changed his major to Mathematics and continued to pursue a degree while working full-time. In his personal time during this period he wrote mainly fiction, started smoking cannabis, and had temporary psychedelic experiences which began to shape his worldview further. 

As he got older, RAW experimented with different philosophical identities. He hopped from one label to the next, one ontology (a metaphysical:philosophical framework for understanding the nature of reality and how it works) to another. Nothing seemed like the right fit, but that wasn’t enough to discourage him from searching for answers to life’s big questions. Eventually he put himself into psychoanalysis during his early twenties to reconcile his Catholic upbringing with the personal views of reality he developed in his time away from his family. Eventually he developed a sturdy mental foundation on which to build. Not long after this he was married, and began a new phase in his life.

That phase included building a close friendship with Alan Watts and using psychedelics alongside his regular cannabis smoking habit. Specifically he used peyote, mescaline, and acid, presumably for their known “expansive” effects. Those books he consumed in his youth shaped his personality to be sure, but his life was forever altered once he started this self-described “14 year experiment” on himself. The idea was that RAW could teach himself to change his brain with a combination of practice and force, based on Dr. John Lilly’s idea of “metaprogramming the human biocomputer.” This entire experiment became the subject of his first book, Cosmic Trigger, Volume 1. One of the key takeaways from a reading of Cosmic Trigger is RAW establishing “Maybe Logic.” The idea is simple to understand, but difficult to implement. 

To summarize: belief is the death of intelligence, and so intelligent people should put forth the effort to not believe anything. Belief, being a system of control which has the power to dull both thought and the senses, was something RAW was vehemently against. Considering the parade of religious and otherwise other dogmatic modalities he found himself studying, understanding, adopting, then dismissing, RAW wanted to assert to others the importance that there is no one way to understand the nature of reality. Each of us has the ability to understand our individual ontologies through experience and intelligent consideration. What works for you may not work for anyone else. That’s why the entirety of his writing works for people, “maybe.” In other words, his framework is established on the basis of heuristics. These mental shortcuts use a person’s past history and ability to reason to establish ways of living:reacting to life that are “good enough.” They allow someone to make quick decisions and find answers that seem to fit for the moment and remain malleable so that they can be changed:updated at any time.

During RAW’s “14 year experiment”, Watts informed him of Dr. Timothy Leary and the Consciousness Revolution. RAW must have found great inspiration in Leary’s work, as RAW himself took Leary’s Eight Circuits of Consciousness model as the inspiration:basis for his PhD in Psychology’s dissertation. Though RAW was proud of his work, he wasn’t able to keep his degree in good standing due to the unfortunate closure of his alma mater. In an attempt to make use of his thesis, RAW reworked it into a more palatable form and, just a few years after earning his degree, had this new version successfully published. That published work is known as Prometheus Rising.

In Prometheus Rising (from here on shortened to PR), RAW describes the Eight Circuits model as being split into two halves, right down the middle. Circuits I-IV are “primitive,” he explains. These four parts make up the basis of all civilizations at present. Exposing them exposes us to some fundamental truths about ourselves and the worlds we live in. Circuits V-VIII are “futuristic,” expanding the boundaries of human consciousness to give way to greater understanding of the human race and our place in our multiverse.*

The largest section of this book, the one just ahead of you, is dedicated to the analysis and understanding of PR as interpreted by me. Each part of it will be explained, scrutinized, then reworked in my own understanding . My use for each of the Circuits is, again, my own. i have found what works for me and my internal:external systems. i’ll explain how i have reached these conclusions, how they relate to my understanding of reality, and show using neuroscience and neuroplasticity their locations and functions in a more precise, and hopefully clear, way than RAW does in PR. 

It’s my belief that RAW assumed a certain level of understanding in those who would read his work. Those that don’t have the scientific knowledge implied throughout PR’s pages may not find its inclusion useful. In fact, PR could easily be read without it, interpreting it as something more akin to a theology than an ontology. 

Though i hate to admit it, my initial understanding of PR was as something to be adherent to. This is totally antithetical to the book’s intention, and to RAW’s concept of Maybe Logic as a whole. To clarify, that is no longer my stance and in adding the science of the brain as it is currently understood to the Eight Circuits, i hope to clarify:solidify my position in the following way: science and mysticism are more common bedfellows than one might think. Holding space for both simultaneously is not impossible. 

∞ i’ve come to think of the lessons i learned from PR as ideas i can hold on to and let go of at the same time. To believe and not believe all at once. The more i practice my understanding of reality this way, the more i’ve come to realize that simultaneous denial and acceptance is itself a quantum function of consciousness that acts as a superposition of observation:understanding. 

∞ That is to say: i can never know exactly how reality will react to my understanding of it unless i measure it. The expectations i have for my reality can change the results. If i’m observing some part of my environment, it may or may not change.

∞ For example: i’m sitting at a red light in my car, looking at the light. If i’m observing the light passively, without any expectation for it to change, it may change from red to green in a way that feels fast to me. If i observe the light actively, the mental state from which i observe it can have an effect on the light. 

∞ What if i’m in a rush and want the light to change faster? Does it take the light longer than average to change from red to green because i’m trying to will it to change faster? Or what if i observe the light actively with the expectation it might change at the end of the song i’m listening to? If the light does turn green at that point, has my expectation caused a meaningful connection? Using quantum mechanics as a basis for observations like these, my intuition has created a space for a reaction i can interpret as a measurement.
